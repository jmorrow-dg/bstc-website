2. EU AI Act (2 August 2026)

What Already Took Effect

2 February 2025: Prohibited AI Practices (Article 5)

Eight categories of AI systems became outright banned:

  • Untargeted scraping of the internet or CCTV to build facial recognition databases
  • Emotion recognition in workplaces and educational institutions
  • Biometric categorisation to deduce race, political opinions, sexual orientation, religious beliefs
  • Social scoring systems evaluating people based on social behaviour over time
  • AI deploying subliminal, manipulative, or deceptive techniques to distort behaviour
  • AI exploiting vulnerabilities (age, disability, socio-economic status)
  • Real-time remote biometric identification by law enforcement (with narrow exceptions)
  • Predictive policing based solely on profiling

Also from February 2025: AI Literacy obligations took effect, requiring organisations deploying AI to ensure staff have sufficient understanding of the systems they use.

2 August 2025: General-Purpose AI (GPAI) Model Obligations

  • GPAI providers must maintain detailed technical documentation
  • Must publish summaries of training data content
  • Must comply with EU copyright law
  • Models posing systemic risk (trained with compute exceeding 1025 FLOPs) face adversarial testing and incident reporting within two weeks
  • GPAI models placed on market before Aug 2025 get until August 2, 2027 to comply

What Happens on 2 August 2026

This is when the bulk of the EU AI Act becomes enforceable.

High-Risk AI Systems (Annex III) become fully regulated across 8 domains:

  1. Biometrics and biometrics-based systems
  2. Critical infrastructure management (energy, transport, water, digital)
  3. Education and vocational training (student assessment, admissions)
  4. Employment and worker management (recruitment, performance evaluation, task allocation, termination)
  5. Access to essential services (credit scoring, insurance risk assessment, emergency services)
  6. Law enforcement (evidence reliability, crime analytics, polygraph alternatives)
  7. Migration, asylum, and border control
  8. Administration of justice and democratic processes

Obligations for high-risk AI operators:

  • Conformity assessments completed
  • Risk management systems implemented
  • Data governance and training data quality standards met
  • Technical documentation finalised
  • Logging and record-keeping systems operational
  • Transparency and information provision to users
  • Human oversight mechanisms in place
  • Accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity requirements met
  • CE marking affixed
  • Registration in the EU database
  • Post-market monitoring systems established

Transparency obligations (Article 50) for all AI systems:

  • AI-generated content must be labelled (deepfakes, synthetic text, images, audio)
  • Users must be informed when interacting with AI chatbots
  • Emotion recognition and biometric categorisation systems must disclose their operation

Member State requirements:

  • Every EU Member State must have at least one operational AI regulatory sandbox
  • National competent authorities must be designated and operational

2 August 2027: What comes next

Article 6(1) takes effect: AI systems that are safety components of products regulated under existing EU harmonisation legislation (medical devices, toys, machinery, motor vehicles, etc.) become subject to the Act.

Penalties (Three-Tier Structure)

Violation Maximum Fine
Prohibited practices (Article 5) EUR 35 million or 7% of global annual turnover
High-risk system non-compliance EUR 15 million or 3% of global annual turnover
Misleading information to authorities EUR 7.5 million or 1% of global annual turnover

For SMEs and startups, fines are capped proportionally and authorities must consider economic viability.

Extraterritorial Reach: The EU AI Act applies to any provider placing AI systems on the EU market or whose AI system output is used in the EU, regardless of where the provider is established. Australian companies serving EU customers must comply.

3. United States: No Federal Law, State Patchwork

Federal Level

No comprehensive federal AI law as of April 2026. The landscape is shaped by executive action:

December 2025 Executive Order

  • Revokes Biden-era AI safety EO 14110
  • Establishes an AI Litigation Task Force within the DOJ to challenge state AI laws
  • Directs FTC to issue policy statement on how the FTC Act applies to AI
  • Conditions $42 billion in broadband infrastructure funding on states repealing "onerous" AI regulations
  • Philosophy: pro-innovation, anti-regulation, federal preemption of state law

March 2026: White House National Policy Framework for AI

Legislative recommendations to Congress proposing broad preemption of state AI laws. No comprehensive federal bill has passed yet.

Only AI-specific federal statute: TAKE IT DOWN Act (May 2025), criminalising nonconsensual distribution of intimate AI-generated images.

State-Level Laws (Active and Forthcoming)

Effective January 1, 2026:

Illinois HB 3773: Amends the Human Rights Act to prohibit employer use of discriminatory AI. Requires notice when AI is used for recruitment, hiring, promotion, disciplinary actions. Includes a private right of action.

Texas RAIGA: Prohibits AI for "restricted purposes" (encouraging self-harm, violence, CSAM, unlawful deepfakes, impersonating minors).

California Multiple laws: Frontier AI transparency (models trained >1026 FLOPs), training data disclosure (AB 2013), AI labelling (SB 942). Penalties up to $1M per violation. Governor Newsom signed AI Vendor Certification for state procurement in April 2026.

Effective June 30, 2026:

Colorado SB 24-205: The most comprehensive US state AI law. Applies to high-risk AI making "consequential decisions" in employment, housing, financial services, insurance, education, healthcare.

  • Risk management policy and programme
  • Impact assessments (at least annually)
  • Consumer notification before adverse AI-driven decisions
  • AG-exclusive enforcement, $20,000 per consumer per transaction
  • No private right of action

Note: In March 2026, a Work Group proposed a replacement framework (ADMT) potentially effective January 1, 2027.

4. Australia (D&G Home Market)

Mandatory AI guardrails were abandoned in late 2025. The September 2024 proposal for 10 mandatory guardrails for high-risk AI was dropped. Australia relies on existing technology-neutral laws.

Key 2026 Developments

Launched AI Safety Institute (AUD $29.9 million): Tests AI systems, assesses risks, and recommends targeted reforms.

10 Dec 2026 Privacy Act Automated Decision-Making Amendments:

  • Businesses using a "computer program" (including AI/ML) to make decisions that could "reasonably be expected to significantly affect the rights or interests of an individual" must disclose this in their privacy policy
  • Covers decisions about benefits, contractual rights, access to significant services (healthcare, finance, housing)
  • "Computer program" is deliberately broad: covers rule-based systems, AI, and ML
  • OAIC can issue infringement and compliance notices
  • Civil penalties apply

No AI-specific legislation expected in 2026. Voluntary guidance (AI Ethics Framework, National AI Strategy) supplements but does not compel.

5. Asia-Pacific

Singapore

Live Jan 2026 World's first Agentic AI governance framework (IMDA). Four dimensions:

  • Risk assessment and bounding upfront
  • Technical safeguards (limiting agent access, SOPs, offline fallback)
  • Clear responsibility allocation across the AI lifecycle
  • Ongoing monitoring and accountability

Voluntary, but organisations remain legally accountable for their agents' behaviours.

South Korea

Effective Jan 2026 AI Framework Act: First comprehensive AI law in APAC.

  • Mandatory obligations for "high-impact" AI in 11 domains (healthcare, energy, public services, etc.)
  • Transparency disclosures, impact assessments, risk management
  • Mandatory labelling for generative AI
  • Fines up to KRW 30 million (~USD 22,000)
  • Presidential committee for AI oversight

Japan

Enacted May 2025 AI Promotion Act: Most permissive major economy.

  • No fines, no banned applications, no pre-launch approvals
  • Government can publicly name companies that use AI to violate human rights
  • Encourages voluntary cooperation

Indonesia

Directly relevant for D&G (Bali operations):

  • AI National Strategy 2020-2045 and ethics framework being formalised as Presidential Regulations
  • National Data and AI Ethics Council planned for launch
  • Sector-specific regulations coming for finance, healthcare, and HR
  • Legal liability provisions for AI-caused harm under development
  • Draft frameworks expected to become binding rules in 2026

ASEAN

Updated AI Governance and Ethics Guide (2025) covering generative AI. Seven principles: transparency, fairness, security, reliability, human-centricity, privacy, accountability. Voluntary only.

6. Canada

AIDA is dead. Canada's Artificial Intelligence and Data Act died on the order paper when Parliament was prorogued on January 5, 2025. No replacement AI-specific legislation on the horizon. AI will be regulated through privacy and sector-specific laws.

7. United Kingdom

The UK has deliberately chosen not to enact a comprehensive AI Act. Instead:

  • Five cross-sector AI principles delegated to existing regulators (FCA, Ofcom, ICO, CMA):
    1. Safety, security, and robustness
    2. Appropriate transparency and explainability
    3. Fairness
    4. Accountability and governance
    5. Contestability and redress
  • No dedicated AI regulator
  • AI Growth Lab proposed: cross-economy sandboxes for testing AI
  • AI-specific bill expected no earlier than H2 2026, likely focused on copyright and regulation of powerful models

8. China

World's most granular and enforcement-ready AI regulations, implemented through sector-specific rules:

  • Deep Synthesis Regulations (2022): Comprehensive deepfake rules
  • Generative AI Service Regulations (2023): Content labelling, copyright compliance, security assessments before public release
  • Mandatory AI Content Labelling (September 2025): Watermark labelling of all AI-generated content
  • Amended Cybersecurity Law (January 2026): AI security reviews, data localisation, strengthened penalties
  • High-risk systems (healthcare, education, law enforcement) face mandatory regular audits
  • All AI models must undergo government security assessments before deployment

11. Impact on SMBs & AI Service Providers

Direct Compliance Obligations

  • If serving EU clients or deploying AI in the EU: Full EU AI Act compliance required by August 2, 2026. High-risk system operators need conformity assessments, technical documentation, CE marking, and registration.
  • If serving Australian clients: Privacy Act ADM transparency obligations from December 10, 2026. Update privacy policies to disclose all automated decision-making.
  • If serving US clients: State-by-state compliance analysis required. Illinois (employment AI) and Colorado (consequential decisions) are immediate priorities.

Key Risks

  • SMBs are disproportionately exposed because they often rely on off-the-shelf AI tools without auditing them
  • AI service providers may inherit compliance obligations as "deployers" under the EU AI Act or "developers" under Colorado's framework
  • The US federal preemption push creates uncertainty: state laws may be challenged in court but remain enforceable until struck down
  • Documentation burden is real: impact assessments, risk management programmes, and training data provenance are required across multiple jurisdictions

Opportunity

  • Compliance advisory is a growth market. Multi-jurisdictional complexity creates demand for firms that can navigate it.
  • Singapore's Agentic AI Framework signals that autonomous AI agents are the next regulatory frontier. Early expertise here is valuable.
  • EU AI Act SME proportionality provisions and regulatory sandbox access offer competitive advantage for smaller players who engage early.
